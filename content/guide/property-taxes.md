## Look up a property, assessment, or tax bill

The City of Burton publishes assessing and tax records through **BS&A Online**. You can
search any property and view assessment details, current and past tax bills, building-department
records, and sales history — and pay a tax bill online:

- **[Burton property & tax search (BS&A Online)](https://bsaonline.com/?uid=209)** — Assessing
  Search, Current Tax Search, Building Department Search, Property Sale Search, and online payment.

Records are maintained by the City and updated regularly. For questions about your assessment or
tax bill, contact the City at **(810) 743-1500**:

- **Assessor** (Ann Abbey) — assessed/taxable value, exemptions, property characteristics.
- **Treasurer** (Alice Bryce) — tax bills, payments, due dates.

City offices are at Burton City Hall; see **[burtonmi.gov](https://www.burtonmi.gov)** for hours
and department pages.

## Understanding your tax bill

Property tax in Michigan is calculated on a property's **taxable value**, using a rate measured
in **mills**.

- **One mill = $1 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value.** For example, at a rate of 4 mills, a
  property with $50,000 of taxable value owes $50,000 ÷ 1,000 × 4 = **$200 per year**. (Burton's
  actual city operating millage is shown on the **Finances** dashboard in this app.)
- **Taxable value is not the same as market value — or even the sale price.** When you buy a home,
  its taxable value is set to that year's State Equalized Value (about half of market value). After
  that, Michigan's Proposal A caps how much the taxable value can rise each year (the lower of 5%
  or inflation) until the property is sold, so over time taxable value usually falls below market
  value.
- **Your total rate combines several millages** — city operating, plus schools, the county,
  libraries, and voter-approved millages. The Finances dashboard breaks down the City's portion.

### Why the city rate is below its maximum

You may notice the City's operating millage is levied **below** its authorized maximum. That gap
is largely the **Headlee Amendment** (Michigan Constitution, Article IX, §31): when a community's
total taxable value grows faster than inflation, the maximum millage a local government may levy is
automatically "rolled back" so that property-tax revenue grows no faster than inflation. Voters can
restore a rolled-back rate with a "Headlee override," but absent that, the cap ratchets down.
